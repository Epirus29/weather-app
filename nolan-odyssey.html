Prologue

A horse on a beach, and a song that stops

A nameless singer opens the film with a song about a past that only seemed enchanted. The song carries us to the beach at Troy, where riders find a towering wooden horse the Greeks have left behind. Sinon, the one Greek who stayed to sell the lie, dies under a volley of arrows. The Trojans start dragging their prize toward the gates, and the verse breaks off before it finishes.

Nolan frames the entire film as the gap between that line and its ending.

The present, eight years after the war

Ithaca under the suitors

Nearly everyone assumes Odysseus is dead. Suitors have moved into his palace and are eating the kingdom hollow while circling Penelope. Telemachus, who has never met his father, refuses to accept it. Antinous, the smoothest and most dangerous of the suitors, proposes marriage to Penelope with a plan to cut her son out of the succession entirely.

No one can throw them out. They are guests, and Zeus's law protects guests. That is the trap the whole film sits inside: the rule binds the people who honor it, not the people who exploit it.

GuestsThe suitors overstay, consume, and plot against the house that feeds them.

The present, far from Ithaca

Ogygia, where the story is told from

Odysseus is the pampered prisoner of the nymph Calypso. Visions of Athena keep telling him to leave, though the goddess never physically does anything. Calypso pushes him to remember how he got here, and that remembering becomes the spine of the film. Everything that follows is memory, nested inside her island.

Memory, just after Troy

The cave of the Cyclops

Following a stray sheep into a cave, the crew are trapped by Polyphemus. They blind him and slip out at dawn beneath his flock. Then Odysseus, unable to leave it alone, fires a taunting arrow back into the cave. The blinded giant prays to his father Poseidon for revenge, and the crew begin to wonder out loud whether their king has just cursed the voyage.

BothThe host tries to eat his guests. The guest blinds his host and mocks him on the way out.

Memory

The Laestrygonian coast

On a forested shore the crew find a lone child. Its scream summons giants. One ship is destroyed and dozens of men die. Odysseus makes it back to the water barely alive, with supplies gone and every survivor questioning every decision he makes.

HostsThe giants kill anyone who lands on their shore.

Memory

Aeaea and the pigs

Circe feeds the starving crew a banquet laced with drugs, and one by one they become pigs. Odysseus, away hunting after a near mutiny, discovers it when a wounded animal turns back into a screaming man. He does not fight her. He talks her down, and she gives him a route home: sail north into twilight and ask the dead.

HostCirce drugs the men she has invited to her table.

Memory

Where fire meets ice

The crew sacrifice sheep and the dead come. Agamemnon tells how his own wife murdered him when he came home, a preview of what a homecoming can turn into. Sinon appears, and Odysseus honors what he gave. Then the blind prophet Tiresias gives the warning: Poseidon's anger will follow you, and whatever happens, do not touch the sun god's cattle on Thrinacia.

Memory

Sirens, the deep, and the cattle

Wax in their ears carries the men safely past the Sirens. A creature from the deep takes sailors off the deck anyway, and Eurylochus turns on his king for gambling with their lives. Dead winds strand them, starving, on Thrinacia. The crew break their vow and kill the sacred cattle. Only Odysseus refuses. When they sail, the storm that follows shatters the ship and drowns every man but him.

He washes up on Ogygia. The lotus blurs time and seven years vanish, though Penelope and Telemachus never fade. Calypso finally names the price of leaving: surrender yourself to Poseidon and let the sea decide. He agrees, and the sea carries him to Ithaca.

GuestsThe crew eat what belongs to the god whose island they are stranded on.

The present, Telemachus's thread

Sparta, and a scarred Helen

Telemachus visits Menelaus looking for news of his father. Through Menelaus we go inside the horse, into the sweltering hours the Greeks spent hidden in it. And Nolan lands a quiet twist: Helen has a scar across her face, and Menelaus laughs at the legend of a beauty that launched a thousand ships. Stories warp every time they are retold. The film keeps saying it.

GuestsThe Greeks enter Troy inside something that looked like an offering.

The present

The ambush at Pylos

Antinous learns where the prince went and sends killers after him with orders to bring back proof. Dressed as pilgrims, they butcher Telemachus's companions at a temple. A weathered stranger stops them at the last moment. Odysseus is home.

GuestsMen who come as pilgrims and kill on sacred ground.

The present, Ithaca

The beggar in the palace

Father and son plan it together. Telemachus returns, announces that Odysseus is near, and orders a banquet for every suitor. On the way in, the disguised king stops to comfort Argos, the family dog, who is dying. That tenderness is what gives him away to his son. Telemachus greets him with three words: "Welcome home, stranger."

At the feast the beggar introduces himself as Sinon and needles Antinous until Antinous throws a bowl at his head. A guest has now struck another guest under the host's roof. Zeus's law is broken in the open, and the trap is set.

GuestAntinous strikes a stranger at a table that is not his.

The present, through a closed door

The confession

Before the blood, the film hides its real climax in a quiet scene. Speaking through a door to a Penelope who still believes he is a beggar, Odysseus tells her what actually happened at Troy. Nolan replays the sack of the city, and this time it plays as a massacre.

The same event, told twice. Switch between them.

Ten years of siege and the walls never fell. Then the cleverest man in the Greek army built a gift. The Trojans dragged it inside with their own hands. That night the Greeks came out of it and opened the gates, and the war that had killed a generation ended before dawn. The horse is the trick that every schoolchild knows, the reason the name Odysseus means cunning.

The horse was a truce offering, and a truce is a promise. The Trojans took it in as a guest. What came out of it killed a sleeping city. Odysseus's own men behead a kneeling priestess of Athena beside the goddess's desecrated statue, and the priestess wears the face we have been seeing as Athena all film. Every vision that urged him home may have been his guilt, walking beside him with her face.

He did not blind a Cyclops first. He broke Zeus's law first, at Troy, in the most spectacular way anyone ever had.

GuestOdysseus entered Troy under the sign of a truce and left it a massacre. This is the original break.

The present, the hall

The trial of the bow

Penelope announces a final contest: string Odysseus's great bow and fire an arrow through twelve axe heads. Suitor after suitor fails. Antinous refuses to try. Then the beggar steps forward, strings it without effort, and threads the shot.

The doors are sealed. Odysseus turns the bow on the room. He meant to carry the killing alone and keep his son's hands clean, but when he is surrounded Telemachus joins him, and together they finish it. Antinous is given a slow death and a message for the dead waiting below: their king will sail west to honor them.

The present, or a dying man's mind

Two endings, and the song completes

Arrows stand out of his back after the fight. Instead of taking the throne, Odysseus hands it to Telemachus and announces a last voyage west with Penelope at his side, an exile he chooses for himself to honor the crew he lost. Then the frame splits.

A ship sails west, Penelope beside him.

He bleeds out on the throne room floor, Penelope holding him.

The voyage west and the throne room are shown together, and the film strongly implies the voyage is happening inside a dying man's head, a mercy given to him by the woman who loves him. Then we return to burning Troy, where the horse is torn down, and the singer finishes the verse he abandoned three hours earlier. Stories only survive as song. That is the last thing the film says.

What Zeus's law actually is

The Greeks called it xenia. Nolan's characters call it Zeus's law, and at more than one point define it as the golden rule: treat others the way you would want to be treated. In a world of islands and long sea crossings it was the rule that kept anyone alive on a foreign shore, and Zeus himself was said to punish those who broke it. A stranger at your door might be a god in disguise.

What the host owes

Food, drink, shelter, safety under the roof, and no questions until the guest has eaten.

What the guest owes

Respect for the house, restraint at the table, and a departure before the welcome runs out.

The film's argument runs both directions at once. The suitors are the obvious violation: guests who never leave and eat the house alive, protected by a rule they hold in contempt. But the Trojan Horse is the same violation from the other side. A gift accepted into a city is a guest, and what came out of it was a slaughter. Nolan makes that the original sin of the story, and the collapse of the whole Bronze Age world spreads outward from it. Once the cleverest man alive has shown the rule can be broken, everyone downstream stops pretending to keep it.

The ledger on this page tracks who breaks the law as the story goes on. Hosts break it as often as guests. And the man who comes home to punish the breakers ends the film having broken it from both sides himself, which is why the ending gives him exile instead of a throne.

Where Nolan leaves Homer

The gods never show up

In the poem Athena is constantly on stage, and at the end she personally brokers the peace on Ithaca. In the film she is a vision, and the confession scene suggests she may be nothing but guilt wearing the face of a woman Odysseus's men killed. Hermes, who frees Odysseus from Calypso in Homer, never appears.

The horse is a crime, not a triumph

Homer's Odyssey barely mentions the horse; the full story of the sack comes from Virgil's Aeneid, written centuries later, and so does Sinon. Nolan pulls both in and reverses their meaning. The poem's proudest trick becomes the wound the whole film is about.

Helen has a scar

The most beautiful woman in the world arrives with her face marked, and her husband laughs at the legend. It is the film's cleanest statement of its running theme: the stories we inherit are not what happened.

Antinous runs a hit in person

Homer's suitors plot to ambush Telemachus at sea and fail. Nolan's Antinous sends assassins disguised as pilgrims to a temple with orders to bring back proof of the kill. He is a schemer with hands, not just a mouth.

Odysseus does not get to grow old

In the poem Tiresias promises him a gentle death in peaceful old age. In the film he takes arrows in the back, gives the throne away, and either sails west into exile or dies in Penelope's arms. Nolan does not say which, and has said he never releases alternate cuts, so the ambiguity is the ending.

A rapper sings the frame

The Bard who opens and closes the film is Travis Scott. Nolan has said he sees rap as the closest living descendant of the oral tradition the poem came from: a performer, a crowd, and a story that changes each time it is told.
